Al-Ghazali — The intellect slayer

Reflect

Before turning to the critique of reason, it is worth stepping back into history to observe the extraordinary impact of reason-based governance — even during eras when autocratic rulers strayed from the foundational spirit of the original message.

“Islamic Empire” and the Renaissance of Europe

In its formative era, the policies of the early Islamic civilization were deeply supportive of rational inquiry and empirical observation. In this intellectually fertile environment, scientific learning, critical philosophy, and multi-disciplinary scholarship flourished on an unprecedented scale, producing thousands of polymaths whose discoveries reshaped global knowledge.

A survey of history reveals Muslim scholars leading their fields globally across mathematics, astronomy, chemistry, optics, medicine, economics, and philosophy.

Two foundational pillars of modern mathematics and computing—algebra and algorithm—directly preserve the names and works of pioneering scholars born over a millennium ago: Geber (Jabir Bin Hayyan جابر بن حیّان ) died in 816 A.D. and Algorithmi (Al-Khwarizmi الخوارزمی ) died in 850 A.D. Indeed, many modern scientific terms beginning with the prefix “al-” trace their origins back to Arabic scientific literature. Intellectual giants such as Hazen (Ibn al-Haytham ابن الہیثم ), , who laid the foundations of the modern scientific method and optics; Alberonius (Al-Bairuni البیرونی ), who pioneered geodesy and comparative anthropology; and Avicenna (Ibn-Sina ابن سینا ) whose medical encyclopedias governed European medicine for centuries, created groundbreaking works that continue to resonate across scientific history.

Though much of the original scholarship from that golden age was lost to time, it provided the essential springboard for the European Renaissance. By the close of the eleventh century, scholars across Europe were eagerly translating, analyzing, and building upon the foundational treatises of these Muslim scientists and philosophers, laying the groundwork for the modern scientific revolution.

The Poetic Lament of Muhammad Iqbal

Yet even as these Muslim polymaths served as intellectual towering giants for pre-Renaissance Europe, the Muslim world was gradually abandoning its own culture of critical inquiry. Over generations, Muslim societies disowned their scientific heritage, allowing an extraordinary legacy of rational thought to fall into neglect and oblivion. Inevitably, intellectual stagnation paved the way for societal and civilizational decline.

The visionary philosopher and poet Allama Muhammad Iqbal (1877–1938) powerfully captured this tragedy in his poetic collection Bang-e-Dra (The Call of the Marching Bell):

لے گئے تثلیث کے فرزند میراثِ خلیل
خشتِ بنيادِ كليسا بن گئی خاكِ حجاز

Europe inherited the intellectual legacy of the Muslim world,
And upon it, built the foundations of its own ascendant empire.

واۓ ناکامی متاعِ کارواں جاتا رھا
کارواں کے دل سے احساس زیاں جاتا رھا

How heartbreaking! Our prized scientific heritage was lost—
Worse still, even the consciousness of that loss has vanished from our collective memory.

Al-Ghazali

A decisive turning point occurred with the influential theologian Al-Ghazali [1058 – 1111 A.D]. In his landmark critique, ’Tahafat-ul-Filasafa’_ [ تہافت الفلاسفة ] — ‘The Incoherence of the Scientists’, he mounted a fierce intellectual assault on the rationalist methodology of the polymaths. Many historians argue that this critique marked the beginning of the end for open scientific inquiry across the Muslim world — a dynamic echoing modern debates (in Europe and the United States) where dogmatic movements challenge scientific consensus and empirical methods.

He begins his book with these allegations:

“I have observed that there is a class of people who believe in… intelligence and insight. They have abandoned all religious rituals.. and defy the injunctions of Shariah (ritualistic laws)… for they were born into an un-Islamic atmosphere and their parents were not any better …”

He then continues …

“[they believe] that the mathematical, logical, physical and metaphysical sciences … are the most profound; that their excellent intelligence justifies their bold attempts to discover the Hidden Things (laws of nature via causes-and-effects); and that with … their intelligence and … accomplishments, they reject the authority of shariah laws … and believe that all such things are only sacred lies (lies in the name of sacred people) and (thus) nonsense. … They refused to be content with the religion followed by their ancestors …” (Ghazali)

This is not new, as religious leaders have always been and continue to use such allegations as an excuse for their blind faith.

While Al-Ghazali addresses a wide array of metaphysical disputes, his most devastating critique targeted the bedrock of all natural science: the principle of cause-and-effect.

Note: In medieval scholarship, no distinction existed between science and philosophy. Empirical investigators were termed “natural philosophers.” Thus, reading “philosopher” in historical texts is synonymous with “scientist-philosopher.”

The Challenge to Cause-and-Effect

Early Muslim scientists operated on the firm understanding that nature moves along consistent, dependable pathways: whenever a specific physical cause occurs, a corresponding effect naturally follows. The cosmos, in their view, operates according to invariant laws.

To dismantle this foundational premise of empirical science, Al-Ghazali argued:

“The connection between the cause and the effect is not necessary … Take for instance any two things … burning and contact with fire; light and the rise of the Sun; death and the severance of the head from the trunk (none is due to cause and effect.) …”

“[Scientists] claim that fire is an agent of burning and whenever there is fire, it cannot stop from doing what is in its nature to do (i.e., burn) after it comes into contact with a subject.”

Al-Ghazali illustrated his point with a famous thought experiment: when fire touches cotton, the cotton chars and disintegrates. But how, he asked, can science prove that the fire itself caused the burning? Fire, being an inanimate material object, possesses no inherent agency or intrinsic power to burn. Science merely observes a sequence of events — fire approaching cotton, followed by cotton turning black — and assumes a causal link. In reality, Al-Ghazali asserted, it is God (acting directly or through angelic agency) who creates the blackness and destruction at that precise moment. Fire is merely an inactive backdrop to divine intervention.

While Al-Ghazali’s philosophical maneuver (an early precursor to David Hume’s critique of causality) is brilliant in its logic, his ultimate motivation reveals his theological objective.

For Al-Ghazali, there are no autonomous “hidden laws” or intrinsic properties within nature. God alone is the immediate, direct Actor in every cosmic event, exercising absolute Free Will moment by moment. Under this view (known in philosophy as occasionalism), fire does not burn cotton by virtue of any inherent physical property; rather, God chooses to ignite the cotton each time fire draws near, simply out of habit.

Al-Ghazali feared that acknowledging intrinsic natural laws would lead people to ascribe independent power to physical matter — rendering God redundant. In his eyes, to believe that fire possesses an inherent, inviolable capacity to burn was theological error, threatening the absolute sovereignty of the Divine.

Where Did Al-Ghazali Fail?

In his zeal to safeguard divine omnipotence and preserve the possibility of physical miracles, Al-Ghazali overlooked a fundamental Quranic premise: God’s sovereignty and the invariance of natural law are not mutually exclusive — they are complementary expressions of the same Divine Wisdom.

The crucial distinction that theologians routinely miss is that the physical law of cause-and-effect and God’s sovereign Will are not opposing forces. God, through His ultimate Free Will, instituted and enforced unchanging natural laws upon the cosmos as the ultimate expression of His design.

God could have willed a chaotic creation, granting physical elements the erratic ability to alter their intrinsic properties at random — allowing fire to burn one moment and freeze the next. But across the entire physical universe, only human beings were endowed with the moral responsibility of free agency. Inanimate matter follows a fixed, unyielding divine decree (Sunnatillah).

Furthermore, a design that demands constant, real-time manual intervention — such as overriding physical laws to manually ignite every piece of cotton — implies an imperfect blueprint that requires continuous patching. Perfect wisdom does not create flawed systems that demand ongoing mid-course corrections.

By framing cause-and-effect as a threat to divine authority, Al-Ghazali unwittingly traded a grand, law-governed cosmos for a chaotic world of divine whim — paralyzing the very spirit of rational inquiry that once made the civilization an intellectual beacon for the world.

Conclusion

The tragedy of the post-Ghazalian retreat from reason was not merely scientific; it was deeply theological. By framing the steady, discoverable order of nature as an enemy of faith, traditionalists severed the vital link between empirical observation and spiritual reflection.

The Quran never asks believers to choose between a sovereign Creator and a predictable universe. Instead, it presents the unyielding constancy of natural law as the supreme signature of Divine Reliability. To rediscover that truth is to restore reason to its rightful place — not as a rival to faith, but as the essential sight through which the light of truth is perceived.
