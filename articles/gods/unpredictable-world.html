An unpredictable world

Concept of God

The question of ultimate origins is as vast as the cosmos itself. To grasp how humanity came to be, one would need a temporal viewport—a machine capable of peeling back the layers of cosmic history to the moment before time itself began. In my imagination, I stepped inside such a vessel.

The device transported me to a realm I call the “Pewniverse”—a conceptual cosmos existing prior to our Big Bang. At first glance, this world was eerily familiar: a burning sun hung in the sky, a silver moon drifted through the night, and continents sat beneath vast oceans. But as I observed more closely, a deep and unsettling chaos revealed itself.

The sun did not rise reliably in the east; one morning it burst forth from the west, and the next it crested in the north. Rivers coiling through valleys did not merely flow toward the sea—on whim, their waters reversed course, climbing mountain peaks or drifting sideways into the air. The immutable principles of physics we take for granted were non-existent. In the Pewniverse, the fabric of reality was governed not by fixed laws, but by perpetual randomness.

On one afternoon, I encountered a man named Pewton sitting quietly beneath an apple tree. But no apple fell neatly upon his head. As a stem snapped, one apple shot upward into the clouds, another hovered motionless in mid-air, and a third exploded sideways. Pewton sat in absolute despair, unable to formulate a single equation or discover the law of gravity. In a cosmos devoid of consistency, science was impossible. Without predictable laws, there could be no wheels, no engines, no flight, and no civilization—only perpetual paralysis.

Unpredictable God

One morning, as the sun flared unexpectedly out of the western horizon, a messenger descended with a divine proclamation:

“I am your Lord, the Creator of all that exists.”

“I am the Almighty, and I act purely on divine impulse. I possess the power to command the sun from any horizon, to force rivers up mountain peaks, to launch apples into the sky, and to bring forth children from virgins.”

“Look around you! I twist and shatter the natural order whenever I Will to demonstrate My miracles and My supreme Power. I am a Living God—not a passive watchmaker. Will you not surrender to My absolute control and find peace by abandoning your futile search for fixed laws?”

Unpredictable Nonbelievers

The capricious Will of the Almighty was the obvious driving force behind every event in the Pewniverse, yet virtually no one believed the messenger. And why should they?

Where there is no pattern, there can be no recognition of design. To the inhabitants, reality felt like a chaotic arena where multiple feuding deities battled for control: one god demanding the sun rise in the east, another forcing it to the west; one pushing waters downhill, another hauling them upward. There was no single, coherent hand maintaining order.

Or perhaps, they reasoned, a single Creator, if He was really One, had fallen into a deep cosmic slumber, leaving the universe to wobble on its axis.

Or worse still: perhaps the Creator was awake, but plagued by an existential identity crisis—suffering from divine mood swings that required constant, theatrical displays of rule-breaking just to reassure Himself of His own power.

Our Sweet Predictable World

By contrast, we are privileged to inhabit a cosmos of breathtaking mathematical harmony. Newton and Einstein were not born into a chaotic void, but into a universe where nature follows elegant, discoverable patterns. From the titanic swirl of black holes to the subtle dance of subatomic quarks, reality is woven together by invariant physical laws.

A single, supreme, and infinitely wise Creator instituted these laws to sustain creation. His Will is not a erratic impulse, but an indomitable, unwavering foundation of order.

Predictable Nonbelievers

Yet a curious paradox defines our world. Humans look out at the flawless regularity of nature, take its seamless stability for granted, and then ask: Why doesn’t God break the rules to prove He exists?

Many religious traditions eagerly rise to this demand, offering sacred texts filled with sensational suspensions of natural law. Unaware of the nightmare of the Pewniverse, millions fall prey to these miraculous fairy tales. But why is the human mind so seduced by the supernatural?

The answer lies deep within our psychological history:

First, the terrifying unpredictability of ancient nature—floods, plagues, and eclipses—instilled a profound helplessness in primitive humans. Desperate to survive, they sought to appease the unseen forces through sacrifices and rituals. In turn, manipulative priesthoods and despotic rulers exploited this fear, entrenching their authority over a fragile populace.

Second, millennia of this psychological conditioning have scarred the human subconscious. The concept of a quiet, law-abiding God feels unsatisfying—even repulsive—to a mind raised on fear. An invisible Creator who governs through steady, unyielding natural law lacks the sensational drama of a cosmic “Superman.”

The universe displays its infinite majesty every second, yet primitive intuition insists on a spectacle: How can we trust a Creator unless He proves His power by breaking His own rules?

“Who will believe in a God who does not part the Red Sea, induce virgin births, or teleport prophets across continents overnight?” cries the fearful, unconfident voice within us.

We remain fixated on what we want God to be, rather than perceiving what God reveals through His Creation—a cosmos operating through unwavering laws within a majestic physical order.

Sadly, the primitive mind still yearns for a Macho Deity wearing the crown of a king.

It is often said that “God created man in His own image.” But looking at human history, it would be truer to say: “Men created God in theirs.”
